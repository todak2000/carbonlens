Executive Summary

Overview

The deployment of carbon capture and storage (CCS) is recognised by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change as essential to limiting global warming to 1.5°C. Yet despite strong scientific consensus, the number of operating CCS projects worldwide remains critically below what net-zero pathways require. A significant but under-examined barrier is tool access: the software used to evaluate potential storage formations costs upwards of $160,000 per year in licensing fees, requires dedicated hardware, and assumes specialist expertise that most research groups, regulatory agencies, and consulting firms in emerging storage markets do not have.

CarbonLens is a browser-based CO₂ storage simulation studio that removes this barrier entirely. It delivers validated physics-based simulation, probabilistic risk assessment, geomechanical safety analysis, regulatory permit documentation, and a digital twin registry, all within a standard web browser, with no installation or dedicated hardware required.

The simulator has been validated against 20 years of real injection data from the Sleipner West CO₂ storage project in Norway, achieving 100% alignment across 7 key metrics within accepted scientific tolerance. It covers 16 global formation presets across five continents and generates jurisdiction-specific permit documentation for five regulatory frameworks: US (EPA Class VI), EU (CCS Directive), Australia (NOPSEMA), Malaysia, and Norway.

CarbonLens is developed by Daniel T. Olagunju. The platform implements a MARS machine learning model documented in a peer-reviewed paper by the author (Olagunju et al., 2026), produced independently of this product.

Section 1

The Problem

1.1 The Net-Zero Gap in Carbon Storage

Carbon capture and storage is not a speculative technology. It is a deployed industrial process, operating at scale since 1996 at the Sleipner field in Norway. The science is well-established: compress CO₂ into a supercritical state, inject it into a porous geological formation beneath an impermeable caprock, and it remains trapped underground for thousands of years through a combination of physical, residual, dissolution, and mineral trapping mechanisms.

The International Energy Agency's Net-Zero by 2050 scenario requires approximately 6 billion tonnes of CO₂ to be captured and stored annually by 2050. Current global capacity is approximately 50 million tonnes per year, less than 1% of the required scale. The gap is not principally a matter of geology: suitable storage formations exist across every inhabited continent. The gap is a matter of project development speed.

1.2 The Tool Access Barrier

Every CCS project begins with a screening question: is this formation worth a serious look? Answering that question requires simulation, quantifying how much CO₂ the formation can hold, how the injected plume will migrate, whether the caprock will hold, and whether the project meets regulatory thresholds.

The industry-standard tools for this work, including CMG GEM and Schlumberger Eclipse, cost between $80,000 and $200,000 per year in licensing fees. They require dedicated workstations, specialist training programmes lasting weeks, and IT infrastructure to manage licenses across project teams. This cost and complexity profile means that early-stage CCS feasibility assessment is effectively inaccessible to:

  • University research groups evaluating candidate formations for academic study
  • Small and mid-sized consulting firms without enterprise software budgets
  • Regulatory agencies in emerging CCS markets who need to review project submissions independently
  • Development organisations and national energy companies in Southeast Asia, West Africa, and Latin America, where significant storage potential exists but commercial tool access is limited
The Core Problem in One Sentence

Projects stall not because the geology is unsuitable, but because the tools to evaluate it are inaccessible, and the consequence is a project pipeline that moves far too slowly for a 1.5°C world.

1.3 The Open Source Gap

Free and open-source alternatives exist but require MATLAB or Python expertise, produce no regulatory outputs, and require significant technical setup. They are tools for researchers who already know what they are doing, not screening platforms for the broader community of engineers, consultants, and policymakers who need to engage with CCS as the world scales it up.

Section 2

CarbonLens: The Solution

CarbonLens is a browser-based CO₂ storage simulation studio. It is not adapted from oil and gas software. Every module was built specifically for CO₂ aquifer storage workflows, drawing directly from published scientific literature. It runs in any modern web browser, on any operating system, on any device capable of accessing the internet.

Figure 1. CarbonLens platform overview. The executive overview panel (left) displays a complete summary of simulation outputs across all modules. The Three.js 3D reservoir viewer (right) renders the CO₂ plume in real time.

2.1 Browser-Native Access

CarbonLens runs entirely in a standard web browser with no installation, no dedicated hardware, and no specialist IT setup required. The physics engines are implemented as TypeScript-compiled mathematical models that execute client-side. Formation data is processed locally and never leaves the user's machine, which is relevant for commercially sensitive projects.

The simulator is accessed via a URL. A researcher in Malaysia, a regulator in Nigeria, or a consultant in London accesses the same tool from the same browser with identical capability: no license procurement, no version management, no deployment infrastructure.

2.2 Formation Library and Model Registry

CarbonLens includes 16 pre-loaded formation presets representing active or candidate CO₂ storage sites across five continents: the North Sea (Sleipner Utsira, Johansen, Snøhvit Tubæen), Southeast Asia (Malay Basin, Kasawari, Duyong, North Sumatra Basin), Australia (Otway, Gorgon), North America (Mount Simon / ADM Decatur, Alberta Basin / Basal Cambrian), West Africa (Niger Delta), North Africa and Middle East (Nile Delta, Abu Dhabi Basin, In Salah), and Europe (Rotterdam North Sea). All formation parameters are populated automatically from published field data.

A built-in Model Registry stores pre-calibrated presets for all validated sites. Each preset encodes peer-reviewed field parameters, enabling one-click formation loading without manual data entry. Users can override any parameter manually or import real well data via LAS file, making the platform suitable for evaluating actual site data alongside the standard presets.

Section 4

Platform Capabilities and Physics Engines

4.1 CO₂ Plume Simulation: Numerical VE Solver and Analytical Pressure Model

The primary plume geometry solver in CarbonLens is a per-cell numerical advection engine operating on a 60×60 Cartesian grid (500 m/cell on a 30 km domain). This is implemented as a 2D Vertically Integrated (VE) gravity-current solver (VESolver.ts), which integrates the depth-averaged CO₂ column height field forward in time using an explicit two-point upwind finite-difference scheme. The VE approximation is physically valid when buoyancy segregation is fast relative to injection timescales, a condition satisfied in high-permeability saline aquifers of the type represented in the formation library.

Injection pressure is computed separately using the Nordbotten (2005) two-phase composite radial-flow model. This model correctly accounts for the mobility contrast between the CO₂ plume zone and the surrounding undisturbed brine, using brine hydraulic diffusivity for the far-field Theis pressure propagation and a near-field mobility-contrast correction at the plume boundary (Nordbotten, Celia and Bachu 2005, eq. 8). The Nordbotten model is used for pressure only; plume geometry is governed by the VE numerical solver.

During post-injection monitoring periods, plume migration is modelled using the Hesse et al. (2008) analytical gravity-current solution, which tracks both the leading and trailing fronts of the spreading CO₂ column after injection ceases.

VE Plume Height Equation (Nordbotten and Celia 2006)
φ(1−Swi) ∂η/∂t + ∇·qCO₂ = Qsource
η(x,y,t) = CO₂ column height at each grid cell (m); qCO₂ = depth-averaged Darcy flux (gravity + pressure gradient); Qsource = well injection term. Solved on a 60×60 grid, forward-Euler, dt = 1 year.
Injection Overpressure: Nordbotten (2005) Two-Phase Composite Radial Flow
ΔP(r,t) = (Q·μbrine) / (4π·k·h) × E1( r2 / (4αbt) ) + (Q·(μeff−μbrine)) / (4π·k·h) × E1( rpl2 / (4αbt) )
αb = brine hydraulic diffusivity (k / φμbct); rpl = current plume radius; μeff = CO₂ effective viscosity / kr,CO₂. The second term is the near-field mobility-contrast correction.
CO₂ Density: Span-Wagner Helmholtz EOS (1996)
ρCO₂ = P·MCO₂ / [ Z(T,P)·R·T ]    where    Z = 1 + δ·(∂φr/∂δ)τ
φr = residual Helmholtz free energy; δ = reduced density; τ = inverse reduced temperature. NIST gold-standard reference equation for pure CO₂. Impurities (CH₄, N₂) handled via mixing rules.
CO₂-Brine Interfacial Tension: MARS-IFT Model (Olagunju, in prep.)
σ(T,P,S) = β0 + Σi βi·Bi(xi)
Bi = piecewise linear basis functions (hinge functions): max(0, xi−ki) and max(0, ki−xi). Trained on laboratory IFT measurements spanning reservoir T, P, and salinity conditions. Feeds directly into caprock seal capacity (Section 4.3).
Refs: Nordbotten et al. (2005) Transp. Porous Media 58:339 • Nordbotten and Celia (2006) Water Resour. Res. 42:W01407 • Span and Wagner (1996) JPCRD 25:1509 • Hesse et al. (2008) J. Fluid Mech. 611:35
Figure 3. Simulation panel. Key outputs include total storage, injection pressure, utilisation efficiency, CO₂ density, and solubility. The 3D viewer shows the current plume state. Simulation runs at up to 5x speed and updates the visualisation continuously.

4.2 Storage Screening Engine

Before simulation can begin, the formation must pass a 10-criterion screening assessment based on the Bachu (2003) sedimentary basin screening framework and the NETL (2015) best-practices site characterisation checklist. Each criterion is evaluated in real time as formation parameters are entered, and is classified as green (pass), amber (marginal, acknowledgment required), or red (fail, blocks progression).

The 10 criteria cover: minimum depth for supercritical CO₂ state (800 m), minimum effective porosity (5%), minimum injectivity / permeability-thickness product, maximum salinity for dissolution capacity, caprock presence and integrity, structural geometry suitability, absence of critical fault proximity, minimum formation temperature (31.1°C, the CO₂ critical temperature), adequate pressure regime, and seismicity risk classification. A red result on any single criterion disables the Stage 2 gate and prevents the user from running a simulation, ensuring that only viable formations proceed to detailed assessment.

4.3 Caprock Seal Capacity

The caprock seal capacity module computes the maximum CO₂ column height that the caprock can contain before capillary entry, using the Leverett (1941) capillary entry pressure framework. The entry pressure is computed from the MARS-IFT model output and the user-specified caprock pore-throat radius, linking the machine learning interfacial tension prediction directly to a quantitative operational safety limit.

Capillary Entry Pressure and Maximum Column Height (Leverett 1941)
Pe = (2 · σ · cosθ) / rt
   hmax = Pe / (Δρ · g)
σ = CO₂-brine IFT from MARS model (N/m); θ = advancing contact angle (degrees, default 0 for water-wet); rt = caprock pore-throat radius (m); Δρ = brine density minus CO₂ density (kg/m³); g = 9.81 m/s².
Refs: Leverett (1941) Trans. AIME 142:152 • Schowalter (1979) AAPG Bull. 63:723 • Hildenbrand et al. (2002) Geofluids 2:3

The module outputs maximum column height (m), approximate maximum containable CO₂ mass (Mt), current buoyancy pressure versus entry pressure, and a seal utilisation percentage based on the current simulated column height. Status is classified as adequate, marginal, or insufficient.

4.4 Dissolution Trapping: Fick Kinetic Model with Convective Enhancement

Dissolution trapping is computed using an interfacial-area-limited Fick kinetic model, not a fixed percentage rate. The effective diffusion coefficient is enhanced by the Rayleigh number to account for convective fingering (Neufeld et al. 2010), which accelerates dissolution after an onset time that scales inversely with the square root of permeability. The dissolution capacity is bounded above by the brine-volume solubility limit computed from the Duan-Sun (2003) equation of state, preventing physically impossible dissolution estimates in low-brine-volume scenarios.

Fick Kinetic Dissolution with Convective Enhancement
mdiss(t) = min( 2 Acontact ρbrine Xsat √(Deff t), Vbrine ρbrine Xsat fconv )
Acontact = πrplume² (plume-brine interfacial area, m²); Xsat = solubility from Duan-Sun (2003) converted to mass fraction; Deff = Dmol × max(1, √(Ra/Racrit)); Ra = Δρdissolved · g · k · h / (μbrine Dmol φ); Racrit = 40 (Neufeld 2010). Convective enhancement factor fconv grows from 1 to 3 after onset time tonset = max(2, 50/√kmD) years.
Refs: Ennis-King and Paterson (2005) IJGGC 1:86 • Neufeld et al. (2010) GRL 37:L22404 • Duan and Sun (2003) Chem. Geol. 193:257

4.5 Relative Permeability

The default relative permeability model is van Genuchten-Mualem (van Genuchten 1980), which is used for all standard saline aquifer simulations. Brooks-Corey (1964) is available as an alternative for users who require it for compatibility with specific formation datasets. Both models feed into the Land (1968) residual trapping calculation. The Land trapping coefficient C = 2.5 is calibrated for sandstone; carbonate formations use the same framework with modified reactive fractions for mineral trapping.

4.6 Mineral Trapping

Mineral trapping uses formation-class-specific Transition State Theory (TST) kinetics (Lasaga 1984; Palandri and Kharaka 2004). Carbonate formations (calcite, dolomite) use a reactive mineral fraction of 5%, specific surface area of 0.5 m²/kg, and reference rate constant of 1.55 × 10⁻⁶ mol/m²/s at 110°C. Sandstone formations use 1% reactive fraction, 0.1 m²/kg, and rate constant 1 × 10⁻⁹ mol/m²/s. Surface passivation is modelled using an exponential decay with formation-class timescale (carbonate: 15 yr; sandstone: 25 yr). Mineral trapping is set to zero for simulations with project duration below 30 years; Benson and Cole (2008) establish that mineral trapping is negligible at 20 years and becomes non-negligible only beyond 50 years. The Benson and Cole (2008) screening cap (15% of injected for carbonates; 10% for sandstones) is applied as an upper bound.

4.7 Fault Reactivation: 3D Stress Tensor Analysis

Fault reactivation risk is assessed using a full 3D stress tensor rotation to compute the normal and shear stresses on user-defined fault planes at arbitrary dip and strike. Byerlee (1978) friction is applied to determine the critical pore pressure increase required to trigger slip on each fault. Multi-fault configurations are supported. The output is a critical pressure increase margin for each fault, providing a quantitative safety envelope for injection operations.

Fault Reactivation: Critical Pressure Increase (Byerlee 1978; Streit and Hillis 2004)
ΔPcrit = (σn − Pp) − τ / μf
σn = normal stress on fault plane after tensor rotation (MPa); Pp = initial pore pressure (MPa); τ = resolved shear stress on fault plane (MPa); μf = Byerlee friction coefficient (0.6 for σn < 200 MPa, 0.85 otherwise). ΔPcrit is the maximum pore pressure increase before fault slip initiates.
Refs: Streit and Hillis (2004) Energy 29:1445 • Byerlee (1978) Pure Appl. Geophys. 116:615

4.8 Thermal Effects

CarbonLens models thermal effects by computing the geothermal gradient contribution to reservoir temperature at the specified depth, and applying a Joule-Thomson cooling correction during CO₂ injection. The injection temperature is computed from the wellhead conditions and the in-situ enthalpy drop. These thermal outputs feed into the Span-Wagner EOS density calculation and the MARS-IFT model, ensuring that near-wellbore thermodynamic conditions are correctly represented.

4.9 Hysteresis and Imbibition Trapping (Killough 1976)

The VE solver tracks imbibition hysteresis cell-by-cell. During periods when brine re-imbibes into regions previously drained by CO₂, the Killough (1976) hysteresis model is applied. This model interpolates between the drainage and imbibition relative permeability curves, capturing the snap-off mechanism that immobilises residual CO₂ ganglia and converts mobile plume CO₂ into permanently trapped residual. The Land (1968) trapping coefficient governs the magnitude of residual saturation in each cell. This hysteresis treatment is significant for post-injection monitoring periods, when the trailing edge of the gravity current is retreating and imbibition is active.

4.10 Halite Risk Assessment

Salt precipitation and near-wellbore dry-out are assessed using the Zeidouni et al. (2009) dryout-radius model. Dry CO₂ injected into the formation evaporates formation brine and can precipitate halite (NaCl) crystals in pore spaces near the wellbore, reducing permeability and potentially impairing injectivity. The model computes the dryout radius as a function of injection rate, formation thickness, porosity, connate water saturation, brine salinity, and CO₂ and brine density. Output includes a risk classification (low, moderate, high) and a quantitative dryout radius estimate in metres.

4.11 Heterogeneity Corrections

Formation heterogeneity is characterised by the Dykstra-Parsons coefficient (VDP), a standard measure of permeability variation between layers. When VDP is specified, CarbonLens applies the Shook and Mitchell (2009) sweep efficiency correction, which reduces the effective storage capacity and plume footprint to account for preferential flow through high-permeability layers and bypassing in low-permeability zones. An area-of-review (AOR) heterogeneity factor is also computed and applied to the plume radius, producing a larger and more conservative AOR consistent with the guidance in Kopp et al. (2010).

4.12 Depleted Field Capacity

For depleted gas and oil field formations, CarbonLens uses the gas-replacement volumetric method of Bachu et al. (2007) rather than the DOE Goodman (2011) Cc coefficient framework that applies to saline aquifers. The gas-replacement method computes storage capacity as the void space vacated by gas production between initial reservoir pressure and abandonment pressure, expressed as CO₂ equivalent at in-situ density. P10, P50, and P90 bounds are derived from uncertainty on the gas initially in place (GIIP) and the abandonment pressure.

4.13 Probabilistic Uncertainty Quantification

Storage capacity estimates carry inherent uncertainty because subsurface parameters are never known precisely. CarbonLens quantifies this uncertainty using Latin Hypercube Sampling across the key geological inputs, generating P10 (conservative), P50 (best estimate), and P90 (optimistic) storage capacity bounds in under five seconds. This probabilistic output mirrors the language used in regulatory submissions and investment decisions.

Storage Capacity: DOE Bulk Volume Method (Goodman 2011)
GCO₂ = A · h · φ · NTG · (1 − Swr) · ρCO₂(T,P) · Es
A = formation area (m²); h = net thickness (m); NTG = net-to-gross ratio; Es = storage efficiency factor (Cc), ranging from 0.51% (P10) to 5.5% (P90) per Goodman et al. (2011). CO₂ density is from Span-Wagner EOS at in-situ T, P. NTG is not applied to the pore volume when using the Cc framework, as DOE Goodman Cc coefficients are derived from gross pore volume statistics that implicitly capture NTG effects.
Latin Hypercube Sampling
xij = Fj−1[ (πij − uij) / N ]
N = number of Monte Carlo iterations; πij = random permutation of stratum index for variable j; uij ~ Uniform(0,1); Fj−1 = inverse CDF of input distribution. LHS ensures full coverage of each marginal distribution with far fewer samples than simple random sampling.
Refs: Goodman et al. (2011) DOE/NETL-2011/1440 • McKay et al. (1979) Technometrics 21:239
Figure 4. Monte Carlo uncertainty panel. P10, P50, and P90 storage capacity estimates generated using Latin Hypercube Sampling across porosity, permeability, thickness, and net-to-gross. The convergence curve confirms statistical stability. Results export as CSV.

4.14 Geomechanical Risk Assessment

Geomechanical failure, including caprock fracture, fault reactivation, or induced seismicity, is the primary reason CCS injection projects are shut in or rejected during permitting. CarbonLens calculates the full suite of geomechanical safety indicators: Mohr-Coulomb shear failure criterion for caprock integrity, Maximum Allowable Injection Pressure (MAIP), fault slip potential (via the 3D stress tensor method described in Section 4.7), surface heave estimate, and a safety factor with threshold alerts at Fs below 1.2 (moderate risk) and Fs below 1.0 (failure).

Mohr-Coulomb Shear Failure Criterion: Caprock Integrity
|τ| = c + σn · tan(φ)
c = cohesion (MPa); σn = effective normal stress on failure plane (MPa); φ = internal friction angle (degrees). Failure occurs when the applied shear stress τ exceeds this envelope.
Safety Factor: Caprock Shear Stability
Fs = (c + σn · tan(φ)) / τapplied
Fs > 1.5 = low risk; 1.0 ≤ Fs < 1.2 = moderate risk (alert); Fs < 1.0 = failure. CarbonLens triggers a risk badge and warning at the 1.2 threshold.
Maximum Allowable Injection Pressure: Fracture Gradient
MAIP = σ3 − Ppore    where    σ3 = K0 · (σv − αPp) + αPp
K0 = horizontal stress ratio; σv = vertical (overburden) stress (MPa); α = Biot effective stress coefficient; Pp = pore pressure (MPa). MAIP defines the upper safe injection pressure limit below which caprock fracture cannot initiate (Hubbert and Willis 1957).
Surface Heave: Poroelastic Reservoir Expansion
uz = (1−2ν)/(1−ν) · α · ΔP · h / (2G)
ν = Poisson's ratio; G = shear modulus (MPa); h = reservoir thickness (m); ΔP = injection overpressure. Surface displacement is a regulatory monitoring metric under EU CCS Directive and EPA Class VI rules.
Refs: Hubbert and Willis (1957) Trans. AIME 210:153 • Teatini et al. (2011) JGR 116:B08204 • Zoback (2007) Reservoir Geomechanics, Cambridge
Figure 5. Geomechanical risk panel. Safety factor, fracture pressure, MAIP margin, and fault slip potential are calculated in real time. The risk badge (Low / Moderate / High) provides an instant plain-language summary for non-specialist users and reviewers.

4.15 History Matching

The history matching module allows users to import field observations (pressure measurements and plume area estimates at specific time steps) and optimise the simulation parameters to best fit those observations. Optimisation is performed using the Nelder-Mead simplex method, which minimises the root-mean-square deviation between observed and simulated values across a user-selected set of matchable parameters (permeability, porosity, net-to-gross, and injection rate). This capability is particularly useful for calibrating the simulator against well test data, 4D seismic plume area measurements, or pressure monitoring records from existing injection sites.

4.16 Monitoring and Synthetic SCADA

The monitoring module provides an observation well conformance checker, which evaluates whether the simulated plume at the location of specified observation wells is consistent with the expected containment envelope. A synthetic SCADA data generator produces time-series pressure and temperature records with realistic Gaussian noise, suitable for demonstration, testing of monitoring protocols, or training workflows. This module supports the development of cost-effective monitoring plans, which are a required component of regulatory submissions under the EU CCS Directive and EPA Class VI.

4.17 Regulatory Permit Documentation

CarbonLens generates jurisdiction-specific permit documentation for five regulatory frameworks: US EPA Class VI Underground Injection Control; EU CCS Directive (European Union); Australian NOPSEMA; Malaysia (Petroleum Act framework); and Norway (Petroleum Act / CO₂ storage regulations). The documents incorporate simulation outputs, safety assessments, and site parameters. Producing a preliminary permit application template is reduced from days to minutes.

4.18 Economics and Carbon Credit Assessment

The economics module performs full-cycle project financial analysis: capital expenditure, operating cost, net present value (NPV), and levelised cost of CO₂ storage (LCO₂S). Carbon credit value is calculated against US 45Q tax credit pricing and EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) rates, producing a net revenue estimate alongside the storage cost.

Net Present Value: Full-Cycle Project Economics
NPV = −CAPEX + Σt=1T [ (Rt − OPEXt) / (1 + r)t ]
CAPEX = capital expenditure (well drilling, compression, monitoring); Rt = annual revenue from carbon credits at year t; OPEXt = annual operating cost; r = discount rate (%); T = project life (years).
Levelised Cost of CO₂ Storage (LCO₂S)
LCO₂S = (CAPEX + Σt OPEXt) / mtotal
mtotal = total undiscounted CO₂ stored (tonnes); LCO₂S expressed in USD/tonne CO₂. The denominator uses total undiscounted stored mass, consistent with standard CCUS cost benchmarking practice. Benchmarks: Sleipner ~$17/t, BECCS projects $60–$200/t.
Carbon Credit Revenue: US 45Q and EU ETS
Rcredits = mannual · Pcarbon    where    P45Q = $85/tonne (2026), PETS = market price (EUR/tonne)
mannual = annual CO₂ injection rate (tonnes/year). US IRS Section 45Q provides a tax credit of $85/tonne for geological storage. EU ETS prices fluctuate; CarbonLens uses the current market rate as a configurable input.

Section 5

Scientific Validation: Sleipner Utsira Benchmark and All 16 Formations

5.1 Why Sleipner?

Rigorous validation is the foundation of any engineering simulation tool. Without evidence that the simulator produces results consistent with real-world behaviour, its outputs carry no scientific or regulatory weight.

The Sleipner West CO₂ storage project in the Norwegian North Sea is the world's longest-running industrial CCS monitoring programme. Injection began in September 1996 and has continued for nearly 30 years. The Utsira Sand formation has been monitored continuously using 4D seismic surveys, gravimetric mass balance measurements, and geological well data. This monitoring record has been published in multiple peer-reviewed papers and constitutes the primary public dataset against which CCS simulation models are evaluated internationally.

Matching a simulator against Sleipner is not a trivial benchmark. The formation is geologically complex, comprising nine distinct CO₂-bearing sand layers separated by thin mudstone horizons with heterogeneous permeability and multi-layer buoyancy migration. Any simulator achieving close agreement with this dataset has demonstrated that its physics engines are calibrated to real subsurface behaviour.

Expected Impact

6.1 Accelerating the Global CCS Pipeline

The most direct impact of CarbonLens is time compression. A pre-feasibility screening exercise that currently requires procurement of commercial software, specialist setup, and days of modelling can be completed in minutes. If every CCS project candidate in the world can be screened faster and at lower cost, the global project pipeline moves faster, and the world approaches the gigaton-scale storage capacity that net-zero requires sooner. CarbonLens does not replace final-stage reservoir simulation on committed projects; it answers the first question every project must ask, and it answers it immediately.

6.2 Geographic Equity in CCS Development

The geographic coverage of CarbonLens is deliberate. The 16 formation presets and five regulatory frameworks were chosen to include Southeast Asia, where Malaysia, Indonesia, and Australia are actively developing offshore storage programmes; West Africa, where sub-Saharan nations are beginning to explore CO₂ storage potential; and the Middle East, where major national energy companies are under pressure to deploy CCS at scale. These are precisely the regions where commercial tool access is most constrained and where the pre-feasibility gap is widest. Making simulation accessible in these regions helps ensure that CCS develops as a globally distributed capability rather than a technology confined to well-resourced operators in the North Sea and North America.

6.3 Academic Research and Workforce Development

University research groups studying CO₂ storage face a structural problem: developing expertise in CCS simulation requires using real simulation tools, but real tools require licenses that most universities cannot afford. CarbonLens removes this barrier, enabling students, PhD researchers, and faculty to engage directly with validated physics-based simulation without requiring commercial licenses or specialist infrastructure. This accelerates development of the CCS-trained engineering workforce that the industry urgently needs to meet the scale of deployment required by 2050.

6.4 Regulatory Capacity Building

Regulatory agencies in emerging CCS markets need to evaluate permit applications submitted by project developers. Where the regulator cannot independently verify the developer's simulation, the regulatory process is weakened and approval timelines extend. CarbonLens gives regulatory agencies and their technical staff the ability to run independent screening simulations using the same physics engines, strengthening oversight without requiring commercial software procurement or specialist staff.

The Digital Twin Registry addresses one of the most persistent bottlenecks in carbon markets: verification. 7.1 Product Overview

7.2 The MARS Model: Machine Learning for Interfacial Tension

A central innovation within CarbonLens™ is the MARS (Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines) machine learning model for CO₂-brine interfacial tension (IFT). Interfacial tension governs capillary trapping, the mechanism that permanently immobilises residual CO₂ bubbles in pore spaces after the injected plume passes through a formation. It is one of the most important parameters in storage capacity estimation and one of the least reliably predicted by conventional correlations across the wide range of temperature, pressure, and salinity conditions encountered in global storage projects.

The MARS-IFT model was trained on a comprehensive set of laboratory experimental data and produces IFT predictions with higher accuracy than existing empirical correlations across the full range of CO₂ storage conditions. The model output feeds directly into the caprock seal capacity calculation (Section 4.3) and into the relative permeability and residual trapping framework. A peer-reviewed paper documenting the model development, training, and validation has been submitted for journal publication and is currently under review.

Section 8

Technical Architecture and Simulator Positioning

8.1 What Type of Simulator is CarbonLens?

CarbonLens is not a compositional simulator and not a black-oil simulator. Compositional frameworks such as Eclipse E300 and CMG GEM are designed for petroleum production: they track multiple hydrocarbon components through a cubic equation of state (Peng-Robinson or SRK) and resolve oil-gas-water phase equilibria through flash calculations at every cell and timestep. Black-oil frameworks (Eclipse E100) assume fixed gas-oil ratio behaviour referenced to surface separator conditions. Both frameworks are irrelevant to CO₂ storage, which involves no hydrocarbon phase envelope.

CarbonLens is a purpose-built CO₂/brine two-phase flow simulator. It implements the Duan-Sun (2003) equation of state for CO₂ solubility in brine, the Span-Wagner (1996) EOS for supercritical CO₂ density, and the Fenghour-Wakeham-Vesovic (1998) correlation for CO₂ viscosity. These are the recognised thermodynamic standards for deep saline aquifer CO₂ injection, and are the same physical models used in TOUGH2/ECO2N and the Eclipse CO2STORE keyword module.

8.2 The Two Co-existing Solvers

CarbonLens runs two physics engines simultaneously, each calibrated for a different use case.

8.2.1 3D Saturation Solver

The 3D saturation solver operates on a structured Cartesian grid (nx × ny × nz cells). It uses an explicit forward-Euler finite-difference scheme with CFL-adaptive sub-stepping. Near the wellbore, a temporal sub-stepping scheme equivalent to a local refinement ratio of 4 is applied automatically. The solver resolves the following physical mechanisms at every annual timestep:

  • Injection source term: Peaceman (1978) log-radial weighting, distributing injected volume according to ln(rₑ/r), the same radial flow model used in all major commercial simulators
  • Buoyancy-driven vertical ascent: Darcy flow with vertical permeability, CO₂/brine density contrast, and gas-phase relative permeability via hysteretic Killough model
  • Lateral gravity current spreading: Nordbotten and Celia (2006) gravity current model near the caprock interface; gradient-driven diffusive spreading in deeper layers
  • Capillary pressure: Brooks-Corey drainage curves, with capillary-driven cross-cell flux on all lateral faces
  • Residual trapping: Killough (1976) and Land (1968) capillary hysteresis model, locking residual CO₂ saturation at imbibition onset
  • Solubility trapping: Interfacial-area-limited dissolution with convective mixing enhancement, budget-limited by the Duan-Sun solubility at formation T/P
  • Mineral trapping: Kinetic precipitation model following Lasaga (1984) and Palandri and Kharaka (2004), acting on the dissolved CO₂ fraction
  • Stress-dependent permeability and porosity: Exponential k-stress model (k = k₀ exp(cₚ α ΔP)) with Biot coefficient α = 0.70 and Kozeny-Carman porosity scaling
  • Fault transmissibility multipliers: Applied on all cell faces intersected by defined fault traces, using the same mathematical convention as Eclipse and TOUGH2

8.2.2 2D Vertically Integrated (VE) Solver

The VE solver follows the theoretical framework of Nordbotten and Celia (2006) and Hesse et al. (2008). It assumes instantaneous vertical equilibrium (CO₂ segregates to the caprock due to buoyancy, valid when the vertical equilibration timescale is much shorter than the injection timescale). This reduces the 3D migration problem to a 2D depth-integrated PDE for CO₂ column height η(x, y, t):

Governing Equation (VE Solver)
φ(1 - Sₚᵢ) ∂η/∂t + ∇·q₂ = Qₛᵒᵘᵅᵐᵉ
where φ = porosity, Sₚᵢ = connate water saturation, η = CO₂ column height (m), q₂ = depth-averaged Darcy flux combining buoyancy and Theis pressure gradient terms
Reference: Nordbotten and Celia (2006), Water Resources Research 42, W01407

The spatial discretization uses an upwind finite-difference scheme with harmonic mean transmissibility at cell faces. Timestepping uses CFL-adaptive sub-stepping with a 0.45 safety factor. The Land (1968) hysteresis model is implemented at every cell to track residual trapping during plume retreat. The solver accepts a full heterogeneous permeability field (nx × ny array in mD) and supports Theis superposition for far-field pressure gradient advection.

8.3 The 3D Visualization Layer

The three-dimensional view rendered in the CarbonLens interface is a WebGL instanced mesh rendered via Three.js and React Three Fiber. It is a visualization layer driven entirely by the physics solver state: each grid cell's colour encodes CO₂ saturation, phase state (free, residual, dissolved, mineral), or a petrophysical property (porosity, permeability, salinity). It is not a geomodel imported from Petrel or a separate geometric engine. The colour field updates every simulation year, enabling real-time 3D observation of plume evolution without any dedicated workstation or graphics hardware beyond a standard laptop GPU.

8.5 Documented Limitations and Resolution Status

Scientific credibility requires transparent acknowledgement of model scope. The following limitations applied to CarbonLens at initial release, together with their current resolution status following the v2.0 enhancement cycle.

  • Structured Cartesian grid (partially addressed, v2.0): Corner-point geometry and irregular stratigraphy remain beyond scope. Non-uniform Cartesian spacing now provides up to 3× finer cells near the injection well via cosine-bell refinement. Fault planes are rendered geometrically in the 3D viewer with dip, strike, and sealing colour. Transmissibility multipliers on cell faces remain the flow representation, consistent with TOUGH2/ECO2N.
  • Explicit time integration (addressed, v2.0): An IMPES (Implicit Pressure, Explicit Saturation) solver option removes the pressure CFL stability constraint via 60-iteration Gauss-Seidel per timestep. The explicit scheme remains the default for k < 500 mD; IMPES is recommended for higher-permeability targets or fine grids.
  • No in-situ EOS coupling (addressed, v2.0): A precomputed 20×40 Span-Wagner density and Fenghour viscosity lookup table evaluates per-pressure-point CO₂ density from the spatial pressure field at each simulation year. The 3D HUD reports field-minimum, field-mean, and field-maximum in-situ density, capacity correction factor, and subcritical zone warnings.
  • Isothermal assumption (partially addressed, v2.0): A geothermal gradient input (°C/100 m) and surface reference temperature allow reservoir temperature to auto-compute at midpoint depth and to be applied per pressure-field point via hydrostatic depth estimation. Full energy-balance heat transport remains outside scope.
  • No multisegment wellbore hydraulics (largely addressed, v2.0): Peaceman BHP, hydrostatic wellbore column, and Darcy-Weisbach single-phase tubing friction (Blasius correlation, 4-inch nominal tubing) are computed and displayed live. Each well now supports multiple depth-fraction perforation intervals with individual flow-fraction assignments. Full multi-phase tubing-flow correlations remain a future enhancement.
  • 3D rendering vs geomechanical model (addressed, v2.0): A spatial geomechanical colour mode colours each grid cell by local estimated safety factor derived from local pressure excess. The live HUD geomechanical panel displays Mohr-Coulomb safety factor, MAIP margin, and induced seismicity risk at each simulation year.
  • Grid resolution fixed at design time (addressed, v2.0): Non-uniform cosine-bell Cartesian spacing provides effective near-well refinement without increasing cell count or solver complexity.
  • Stochastic heterogeneity, not geostatistical (remaining): Property fields are spatially correlated via fractional Brownian motion noise. Variogram-based sequential Gaussian simulation or pluriGaussian facies modelling is not implemented and remains a known scope boundary.

Appropriate use statement: CarbonLens is designed for pre-feasibility screening, rapid multi-site comparison, regulatory pre-screening documentation, academic training, and regulatory capacity building. It is not a replacement for full field development simulation on committed injection projects. The appropriate workflow is CarbonLens for screening followed by TOUGH2, Eclipse CO2STORE, or CMG GEM for final-stage certification where required by the relevant regulatory body.
